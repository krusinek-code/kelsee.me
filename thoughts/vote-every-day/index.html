YOUVOTEevery day.

The ballot isn't the only place your choices matter. An editorial experiment about pride, principle, and the world we create.

01 / A question

Do you believe you're changing the world?

I do.

Not because I believe everything I create will be revolutionary. Not because I expect everyone to agree with me.

But because I believe the decisions we make change the world around us. Every single day.

And with Election Day approaching here in the United States, I've been thinking about what it actually means to vote.

Voting matters. It's an important way we participate in shaping our communities and our future. But wherever we live, our choices reach beyond elections.

Every decision is a vote for the kind of world you're helping create.

02 / The mirror

We spend a tremendous amount of time listening to political figures. Their words fill our ears. Their arguments enter our conversations. Their conflicts become our conflicts.

We hear them at work. At home. In our social feeds. In conversations with people we love.

They can make us angry. They can occupy our thoughts. They can take away our peace, especially when we allow their behavior to dictate our own.

And sometimes, without realizing it, we begin reproducing the very behaviors we find objectionable.

When do you stop listening because you're convinced you're right?

When do you dismiss someone because they disagree with you? When do you make assumptions about someone's character based on a single opinion?

When do you defend your position so fiercely that you forget to examine your behavior?

Can you recognize those moments in yourself? And, more importantly, can you change them?

What we defend
PRIDE.

We can become so invested in defending our self-image that we stop examining our behavior.

What we practice
PRINCIPLE.

Our values become visible when living by them requires us to set aside our comfort.

03 / Accountability

What happens when pride becomes more important than principle?

We can believe we're compassionate while treating people without compassion.

We can believe we respect independence while dismissing another person's judgment.

We can believe in freedom while trying to control the choices of those around us.

We can believe we're changing the world while making decisions designed primarily to preserve the one we're comfortable living in.

That's a fascinating contradiction. And it's not exclusive to politics. It exists in our workplaces, our communities, our friendships, and our closest relationships.

Can you admit you were wrong? Can you respect someone's autonomy when they make a choice you wouldn't make yourself? Can you protect something important even when doing so requires you to sacrifice your own comfort?

And can you recognize when your actions have hurt someone without making their reaction the problem?

Accountability isn't simply admitting that something went wrong. It's being willing to change what happens next.

Changing the world requires being willing to discover that you're part of what needs changing.
04 / The architecture of a decision

Every decision has an architecture.

As an artist and technologist, I spend a lot of time thinking about systems. How they're built. What assumptions support them. What happens when those assumptions go unquestioned.

What happens when we change one part of the architecture and discover possibilities we couldn't see before.

People have systems, too. We develop patterns of thought and behavior. We inherit ideas about authority, loyalty, success, identity, and what constitutes being right.

And sometimes we defend those patterns simply because they're familiar.

But familiarity doesn't make something fair. Tradition doesn't automatically make something right. And confidence doesn't make someone correct.

The beautiful thing about systems is that they can evolve. We can examine our assumptions. We can recognize contradictions. We can change our behavior. We can build something better.

The pressure to choose a side

I've been reflecting on the pressure people experience to identify with one political group or another. I'm independent in how I approach these questions, and fairness matters enormously to me.

But I've also experienced how quickly political disagreement can become personal. How easily someone's position becomes a substitute for understanding who they are. How quickly curiosity can disappear when we believe we already know what another person represents.

And I've found myself questioning what meaningful participation looks like when the pressure to belong to a side begins to overshadow the principles we're supposed to be discussing.

I'm still considering what participation means for me this election. But I know what I would like to see more of.

People willing to question their own assumptions. People willing to acknowledge contradictions in their own thinking. People who can disagree without diminishing someone else's humanity. And people willing to put fairness ahead of pride, even when doing so is uncomfortable.

Because I don't think the most important question is always which side you're on. Sometimes it's whether you're willing to examine what your side—and you yourself—might be getting wrong.

05 / What we make possible

Changing the world starts closer than we think.

You don't have to invent something revolutionary. You don't have to hold public office. You don't have to convince the entire world to agree with you.

Sometimes you change the world by listening when you'd rather argue. By recognizing someone else's potential. By encouraging an idea you don't fully understand. By admitting you were wrong.

By refusing to diminish someone simply because their perspective challenges yours. By protecting someone's autonomy, even when you disagree with how they exercise it. By making a different decision today than you made yesterday.

That's how change happens, too. Not just through enormous movements or historic breakthroughs. But through the accumulation of ordinary decisions that shape the lives of the people around us.

So, do you believe you're changing the world?

I do.

I believe art can change how we see things. I believe technology can expand what we're capable of creating. I believe curiosity can open doors that judgment keeps closed.

And I believe we have more influence over the world immediately around us than we sometimes recognize.

I create because I'm curious about what's possible. I build because I want to understand how things work—and what happens when we imagine them working differently. I share my ideas because another perspective, even one we don't agree with, can change what we're capable of seeing.

Not to tell people what to think, but to encourage curiosity, imagination, independence, and the freedom to explore possibilities beyond the ones we've been handed.

Because changing the world doesn't always mean convincing someone to agree with you. Sometimes it means giving them room to become more fully themselves. And sometimes it means having the courage to change yourself.

06 / Your next vote

So yes, voting matters. Participate. Pay attention. Think critically. Make your own informed decisions.

YOU VOTE EVERY DAY.

With your voice. With your actions. With your choices. And with your willingness to change when those choices no longer reflect the person you believe yourself to be.

The question isn't only whether you believe you're changing the world.

It's whether you're willing to let the world change you, too.
